A nameplate is the smallest piece of decor with the biggest job. It is the first thing a guest reads, the detail a courier squints at, the quiet announcement that this door is *yours*. So when people commission one, the question that matters most is rarely asked out loud: should it be 3D printed, or cast in resin? Both can be beautiful. Both can be personalised down to the letterform. But they are genuinely different objects — in weight, weather resistance, finish and price — and the right choice depends on your door, not on the technique. Here is the honest comparison.

## What each one actually is

**A 3D printed nameplate** is built layer by layer from durable plastic — usually PLA for indoor doors and PETG for exposed ones. The design exists first as a digital file, which means any font, any motif, any size, and geometry as crisp as typography on a screen. Raised letters, cut-out letters, layered two-colour designs and integrated brackets are all natural to the medium.

**A cast resin nameplate** is made by pouring liquid epoxy into a mould and working the surface by hand — pigments swirled into marble or agate patterns, flowers or gold leaf embedded, letters set in or laid on top, the whole piece cured into a dense, glassy slab. It is closer to a small painting that happens to carry your name.

The two are cousins to each other the way a tailored shirt is cousin to a hand-embroidered one: same purpose, different souls.

## Look and feel: crisp versus deep

Hold one of each and the difference is immediate.

- **The printed plate** is light, precise and architectural. Edges are sharp, letters geometrically perfect, and finishes range from soft matte to silk sheen. Its beauty is in its exactness — it looks *designed*.
- **The cast plate** is heavy, cool and glossy, with colour that appears to sit centimetres deep inside the surface. No two pours are alike; the marbling on your plate exists nowhere else on earth. Its beauty is in its uniqueness — it looks *made*.

If your home leans modern, minimal or Scandinavian, printed precision often sits better. If it leans warm, artistic or traditional — or if you want real flowers, brass flakes or a colour story sealed inside — cast resin is hard to beat. ## Durability and weather: the question that decides most orders

This is where the honest answers matter, because a nameplate's location does more to determine the right technique than taste does.

**For a sheltered door** — inside a building corridor, under a deep porch, in an apartment lobby — both options live long, comfortable lives. Choose on aesthetics and budget alone.

**For an exposed door or gate** — direct sun for hours, monsoon rain, coastal air — the picture changes:

- Standard PLA prints will eventually soften and warp in harsh direct sun; PETG prints handle heat and rain far better and are the sensible printed choice outdoors.
- Cast resin is dense and completely waterproof, and quality UV-resistant epoxy holds up respectably — but *any* resin exposed to relentless afternoon sun for years will slowly amber. Placement under even a small overhang extends its life dramatically.
- Fixing matters as much as material: a plate mounted flush to a wall that heats up all afternoon lives a harder life than one on standoff spacers with airflow behind it.

The practical rule: full, brutal, all-day sun favours a PETG print or a resin plate with genuine shade; anything sheltered opens every door.

## Practicalities: weight, mounting and price

- **Weight:** printed plates are light (often 100–300 g) and can hang on strong adhesive strips or two small screws. Cast resin plates are substantial — a medium plate can weigh over a kilogram — and want proper screws or anchors. That heft feels premium in the hand but demands honest mounting.
- **Repairs and changes:** a printed design can be re-produced identically if damaged, since the digital file persists. A cast plate can never be exactly repeated — which is either its flaw or its entire point, depending on your temperament.
- **Lettering longevity:** printed letters are the same material through and through. On cast plates, insist on letters embedded under or into the resin rather than stuck on top, so they can never peel.
- **Timelines:** printed plates typically finish in a few days; cast resin needs pour, cure and finishing time — usually one to two weeks. Our [process page](/process) explains why cure time refuses to be hurried.

### Price, honestly

As broad guidance for made-to-order work in India: printed nameplates generally begin in the high hundreds of rupees and rise with size and multi-colour complexity, while cast resin plates typically start around one and a half to two thousand and climb with size, embedded elements and detailing. Neither price buys a better object than the other — they buy different objects. A large, layered printed design can cost more than a simple cast plate; an elaborate floral resin piece with brass letters can cost several times a basic print.

## The third option: why choose at all

The quiet secret of this comparison is that the best nameplates increasingly refuse to pick a side. A printed frame provides perfect letterforms and a crisp border; hand-poured resin fills the field behind them with ocean waves, marble or a colour matched to your front door. You get typographic precision *and* one-of-a-kind depth in the same plate. If both columns of this article appealed to you, the hybrid route through a [custom order](/custom-order) is probably your answer.

## A simple decision path

- **Exposed to harsh, all-day sun and rain?** PETG print, or cast resin with real shade.
- **Modern minimal home, tighter budget, faster timeline?** 3D printed.
- **Want embedded flowers, deep gloss, a piece no one can duplicate?** Cast resin.
- **Want perfect lettering and organic artistry together?** Hybrid.
- **Still torn?** Send a photo of your door — the space usually answers the question within a minute.

Whichever way you choose, insist on seeing the design proof before making begins, and tell your maker honestly where the plate will live. A nameplate is read every single day for years; it deserves ten minutes of honest deciding.
