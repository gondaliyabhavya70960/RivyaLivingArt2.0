Ask any parent of a ten-year-old what they wish they had kept, and the answer is rarely the toys or the tiny clothes. It is the measurements of a person who no longer exists at that size: the foot that fit in a palm, the hand that wrapped around one finger. Babies outgrow their own footprints in a matter of weeks — which is precisely why a print, taken once and preserved well, becomes one of the most moving objects a family can own. Resin, with its glass-like depth and permanence, is one of the finest ways to keep it.

This is a guide to doing it thoughtfully: what to preserve, how to capture it safely, and how to keep the result elegant rather than sentimental-in-the-wrong-way.

## Why Resin Suits Baby Keepsakes

Paper prints fade and crease. Clay impressions chip and gather dust in a drawer. Resin solves both problems in one pour: the print, photograph or tiny memento is sealed inside a solid, polished, waterproof block or frame that needs no maintenance beyond an occasional wipe. Kept out of harsh direct sunlight, a well-made piece will still look freshly poured when the baby in question is choosing a college.

There is also an aesthetic argument. Resin's transparency lets a keepsake feel light rather than heavy — a footprint floating in a clear block reads as art, not clutter. And because every piece is made to order, the palette can match the nursery, the sibling's existing keepsake, or the parents' home rather than defaulting to pastel pink and blue.

## What to Preserve: Ideas Beyond the Footprint

The footprint is the classic, but the first year offers a surprising number of preservable moments:

- **Footprint or handprint art** — an ink or paint print, scanned or taken on paper, set into a resin frame or block with the baby's name and date of birth.
- **The hospital identity band** — small, humble, and unbearably evocative years later. It embeds beautifully alongside a first photograph.
- **The announcement details** — name, date, time and birth weight set in elegant lettering, sometimes with a preserved flower from the hospital bouquet.
- **A naming-ceremony or annaprashan memento** — a keepsake marking the naamkaran or first rice ceremony, with the chosen name as the centrepiece.
- **First birthday markers** — a print taken at exactly one year next to the newborn print tells the whole story of that astonishing first year in one object.
- **A nursery name plaque** — less a keepsake than a piece of the room itself; personalised plaques and pieces from a [kids' room collection](/shop/kids-room-decor) grow with the child rather than being packed away.

One quiet suggestion from experience: make two of whatever you choose. Grandparents feel the passage of that first year even more keenly than parents do, and a duplicate keepsake is among the most treasured gifts they will ever receive.

## Taking a Good Print at Home, Safely

The keepsake can only be as good as the print, so it is worth doing carefully. Important note first: the baby's foot or hand never touches resin itself — you capture the print with skin-safe materials, and the artisan preserves the result.

- **Use baby-safe, non-toxic ink or paint** made specifically for infant prints, or an inkless print kit where the baby touches only a clean wipe and the print appears on treated paper. Never use standard stamp ink or craft paint on infant skin.
- **Time it around sleep.** A feeding-drowsy or sleeping baby has relaxed, open hands and still feet. An alert baby has fists of iron and opinions.
- **Feet are easier than hands.** Start with the foot; attempt the hand only if the session is going suspiciously well.
- **Take several.** Do five or six prints and choose the cleanest. Smudged first attempts are the rule, not the exception.
- **Newborn prints are tiny — act early.** The most treasured pieces tend to come from prints taken in the first six weeks, when the scale is at its most disarming.

If a print session has already been missed, all is not lost: a high-resolution photograph of an existing hospital print, or even a clear photo of the baby's foot, can often be worked into a design.

> A footprint is the only portrait that measures exactly how small they once were.

## Keeping It Tasteful: The Restraint Principle

The difference between an heirloom and a novelty is almost always restraint. A few principles that consistently produce keepsakes parents still love a decade on:

- **One hero element per piece.** A footprint *or* the hospital band *or* a photo as the focus — not all three shouting at once. Supporting details (name, date) should whisper.
- **Muted, grown-up palettes.** Ivory, soft sage, dusty blush, warm sand, or clear resin with a thread of gold outlive nursery-brights. The keepsake will one day sit in a living room, not a cot-side shelf.
- **Real names, real dates, no slogans.** The baby's name and birth date carry more feeling than any pre-written caption ever will.
- **Design for the ten-year view.** Ask of every element: will this still feel elegant when the child is ten? Cartoons date; typography and flowers do not.

A capable artisan will guide these choices — this is exactly the kind of conversation worth having before anything is poured, and it is why a made-to-order [custom commission](/custom-order) beats anything pre-designed for a gift this personal.

## Gifting a Keepsake: For Whom, and When

Milestone keepsakes make extraordinary gifts precisely because new parents never think to commission one for themselves — the first year is a blur, and by the time they surface, the tiny-print window has closed. If you are the friend, sibling or grandparent who arranges it, you have given something no one else thought to give.

Natural gifting moments: the naamkaran or naming ceremony, the annaprashan, the first birthday, or simply the end of the fourth trimester, when parents finally exhale. For baby showers, consider gifting the *promise* of the keepsake — an elegant card explaining that a print session and preserved piece await, once the baby arrives and the family is ready. It removes all pressure and adds anticipation.

On practicalities: allow two to four weeks for a made-to-order piece once the print or materials are shared, since layered resin work cures in stages — the making itself is a considered, unhurried craft, as any look at a studio's [process](/process) makes clear. Small keepsake blocks and frames generally begin in the low thousands of rupees, with larger framed compositions and multi-element pieces above that.

Children grow; that is the whole point of them. The privilege of this craft is making sure that one small, perfect measurement of who they were — ten toes, one date, a name newly given — never grows another millimetre.
