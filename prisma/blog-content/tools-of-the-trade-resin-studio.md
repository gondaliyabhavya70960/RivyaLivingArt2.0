People imagine a resin studio the way films imagine artists' studios: dramatic, splattered, ruled by inspiration. The reality is closer to a kitchen run by a very fussy chef. The most-used object is a weighing scale. The most feared is a humidity meter. And the tool that shapes a piece more than any other costs a few rupees a sheet. Here is an honest walk along the workbench — what each tool actually does, and why the unglamorous ones matter most.

## The scale: where every piece secretly begins

Resin is a two-part chemical system, and the ratio between resin and hardener is not a suggestion — it is the recipe for the reaction itself. Too much hardener and the cure runs hot and brittle; too little and the piece never fully hardens, staying faintly tacky forever. There is no fixing an off-ratio mix after the fact. None.

So the digital scale, accurate to a fraction of a gram, is the first and last word on every pour. Pigments are weighed too — a recorded formula is the only way a client's approved sample colour can be faithfully reproduced in the final pour weeks later. It is the least romantic object in the studio and the one nothing works without.

## Mixing tools, and the enemy called air

Every bubble you have ever seen trapped in cheap resin work was stirred in by someone in a hurry. Mixing resin is a discipline: slow, deliberate folding with flat stirrers, scraping the sides and base of the cup repeatedly — unmixed resin hiding at the walls of a cup is a classic cause of soft spots — and then, for critical pours, letting the mix rest so the air stirred in can begin rising out.

Some studios add a vacuum or pressure step for flawless deep castings; all studios rely on the same fundamentals. Patience at the mixing cup is invisible in the finished piece — which is exactly the point.

## Heat: the torch, the gun and the difference between them

Two heat tools live within arm's reach of every pour, and they do completely different jobs:

- **The butane torch** kisses the surface of fresh resin for a second at a time, thinning it just enough for risen bubbles to burst. It is also the wave-maker — the controlled heat that drives white pigment into the branching lace of an ocean pour.
- **The heat gun** moves air as well as warmth, pushing colours across a surface to create sweeps, cells and marbling that no brush could draw.

Both are five-second tools with ten-year learning curves. Too little heat and bubbles survive; a moment too much and the resin scorches, ripples or yellows. Watching an experienced hand flick a torch across a pour — never pausing, never returning to the same spot — is watching most of the craft happen in silence.

## Moulds: the negative space of the craft

Every cast piece is born from a mould, and the mould decides more about the outcome than almost anything else. Food-grade silicone moulds give the mirror-gloss faces on [coasters and trays](/shop/resin-trays-serving-platters); rigid formwork sealed with care builds the one-off channels for river tables; and custom moulds — made in the studio for a single commission — are how a [bespoke piece](/custom-order) gets a shape no catalogue offers.

Moulds are also consumables, which surprises people. Silicone tires with every casting, slowly losing its gloss and crispness, and a studio that keeps using a fogged mould ships fogged pieces. Retiring moulds on time is one of those quiet costs of quality that never appears in a photograph.

### Where 3D printing joins the bench

Increasingly, the mould-making chapter starts on a printer. A form is designed digitally, printed precisely, and used to cast a fresh silicone mould around it — which means a client's idea can become a completely new shape without a factory involved. It is the same marriage of technologies behind our [3D printed decor work](/shop/resin-home-decor): digital precision serving handmade character.

## Sandpaper: the humblest tool does the most work

Ask what tool most transforms a piece and the honest answer embarrasses everyone: sandpaper. A demoulded casting has sharp flash on its edges and the faint texture of its mould on its faces. Between that rough object and the glass-finished piece in your home lies the grit ladder — sanding through six, eight, ten successively finer grades, each stage erasing the scratches of the one before, usually wet, always by hand on the surfaces that matter.

Skip a grit and the final polish will magnify the shortcut as a haze of swirl marks under the gloss. There is no drama to this stage, no reel-worthy footage — just hours of patient, rhythmic work that decides whether a piece feels crafted or merely cast.

> The torch gets the applause, but the sandpaper does the work. Every luxurious edge you have ever run a thumb along was earned one grit at a time.

## The polisher, and the quiet instruments

The last machine a piece meets is the polisher — a buffing wheel and fine compound that raise the sanded surface to its final depth of gloss. And around all of these headline tools sits the supporting cast that never gets mentioned: the hygrometer that decides whether today is even a pouring day; the spirit level that keeps a curing surface true (resin finds every slope and records it forever); the timers running through every session; and the notebook — formulas, layer schedules, cure logs — that lets a studio reproduce an approved sample instead of merely resembling it.

In the end, though, the tool list is not the craft. Everything above can be bought in an afternoon. What cannot be bought is the judgement threaded through it — knowing this resin, in this weather, at this depth, needs the torch now and not in ninety seconds. Tools set the ceiling of what is possible; hands and habits decide what actually happens. When you commission a handmade piece, that judgement is the thing you are really paying for — the tools are just how it reaches you.
