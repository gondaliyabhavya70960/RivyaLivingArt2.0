There is a particular sadness in watching a once-glassy resin piece go quietly dull — the depth flattens, the colours lose their wet-look luminosity, and the piece starts to look like a photograph of itself. The comforting truth is that dullness in resin is almost always a *surface* condition. The beauty is still there, a fraction of a millimetre down. Whether you can bring it back at home or should hand it to the studio depends on what caused the dulling in the first place — so let us start there.

## Diagnose before you polish

Run a clean fingertip across the surface in good light and look closely:

- **Uniform haze, surface feels smooth** — this is the most common case: accumulated micro-scratches from dry dusting, plus soap film or hard-water residue. Very recoverable at home.
- **Fine visible scratches, catch the light at an angle** — light abrasion damage from grit or dragging. Usually recoverable with careful polishing.
- **A single deep scratch or gouge you can feel with a fingernail** — beyond home polishing. Polishing around it will only create a dip; this needs professional attention.
- **Yellowing or ambering, especially in clear areas** — this is a chemical change from UV exposure, not a surface film. No amount of polishing removes it. A studio can sometimes recoat or rework a piece, but honest expectations matter: yellowing is largely permanent.
- **Sticky or tacky patches** — not a gloss problem at all; see the studio. Tackiness on an old piece suggests surface contamination or, rarely, a cure issue that home remedies will worsen.

If you are unsure which of these you are looking at, photograph the piece in daylight and ask before doing anything. Guessing wrong with abrasives is the one mistake that cannot be undone at home.

## Level one: the deep clean most pieces actually need

Before any polishing, eliminate the impostor: residue. A surprising number of "dull" pieces are simply wearing months of soap film, incense smoke, kitchen grease vapour or hard-water spotting.

1. Wash the piece with **lukewarm water and a drop of mild dish soap**, using only a soft cloth.
2. Rinse thoroughly — residue from the cleaning itself is a common cause of streaking.
3. Dry completely with a fresh microfibre cloth, then buff with a second dry one using light, brisk strokes.

Assess in daylight. If the gloss is back, stop — you are done, and you have risked nothing. This resolves perhaps half of all dull-resin complaints on its own.

## Level two: gentle home polishing for haze and micro-scratches

If the piece is clean but still hazy, you can micro-polish — carefully.

**What you need:**

- A **plastic-safe polishing compound** — a fine automotive finishing polish or a dedicated acrylic/plastic polish. These are engineered to refine clear coats, which is chemically close to what a resin surface is.
- Two or three clean microfibre cloths.
- Good daylight and patience.

**The method:**

1. Work on a **small test area first** — the back, the base, an edge that does not show. Never begin in the middle of the face.
2. Apply a pea-sized amount of polish to the cloth, not the piece.
3. Polish in **small overlapping circles with light pressure**. Let the compound do the work; pressing harder does not polish faster, it only heats the surface.
4. Work one palm-sized area at a time, then wipe clean with a fresh cloth and inspect before moving on.
5. Finish with a wash in mild soapy water to remove all compound residue, then dry and buff.

For flat glossy faces — a [vanity mirror](/shop/vanity-mirrors) frame or a table surface — this method can be genuinely transformative. For textured, matte-finished or heavily dimensional [art pieces](/shop/art-craft-pieces), skip home polishing entirely: compound lodges in texture and a deliberate matte finish will be ruined, not revived, by polishing.

> Polishing removes material. Every pass takes a little of the surface with it — which is why the lightest touch that works is always the right one.

**What never to use, even when the internet suggests it:**

- Sandpaper of any grit, unless you are prepared for a full professional-style refinish — sanding without a recoat leaves the piece worse.
- Toothpaste — unpredictable abrasives, inconsistent results, and a residue that is difficult to fully clear.
- Metal polishes like Brasso — formulated for metal oxides, too aggressive and chemically wrong for resin.
- Buffing attachments on drills at high speed — friction heat can soften and smear the surface in seconds.
- Furniture wax or oil as a "shine restorer" — it fakes gloss briefly, attracts dust, and complicates any future proper restoration.

## When the studio is the right answer

Some conditions deserve professional hands, and knowing when to stop is part of caring well:

- **Deep scratches or chips** that catch a fingernail.
- **Yellowing** you want addressed through recoating or rework.
- **Heat impressions** — the ghost ring from a hot vessel is a deformation, not a scratch; it needs filling or recoating.
- **Pieces with inclusions close to the surface** — varmala blocks, photo pieces, gold leaf work — where over-polishing risks breaking through to the precious layer beneath.
- **Any piece you would grieve.** If the thought of a polishing mistake genuinely worries you, that is your answer. An heirloom deserves the same hands that made it.

A professional revival typically means cleaning, careful surface refinement, and a fresh flood coat of resin — effectively giving the piece a brand-new glass skin. It is how a decade-old piece comes back looking newer than your memory of it. ## The habit that makes revival rare

Everything in this guide becomes less necessary with one change: retire the dry dust cloth. Dry-wiping a dusty surface is the slow, invisible source of most haze. A barely-damp microfibre cloth, once a week, is the entire secret to a piece that never needs level two at all.
